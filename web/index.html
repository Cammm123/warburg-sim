What you are looking at

Healthy tissue (green) is fed by capillaries (red dots) that supply oxygen and glucose and carry acid away. A small tumor starts in the middle. Each step, every cell eats, makes energy (ATP), and then dies (too little ATP, or too much acid), stays, or divides into an empty neighboring square.

Every cell solves Sv = 0

A cell's chemistry is a matrix S: one row per molecule (glucose, pyruvate, NAD⁺, NADH, lactate), one column per reaction. A flux vector v says how fast each reaction runs, and a cell in balance needs Sv = 0. The solutions are the null space of S. Row reduction shows it is two-dimensional, with a whole-number basis of two pathways:

ferment = (1, 1, 0, 2, 0, 2): 1 glucose → 2 lactate, 2 ATP
respire = (1, 1, 2, 0, 2, 0): 1 glucose → CO₂, 32 ATP, uses 6 O₂

So every cell is a point a·ferment + b·respire, and it picks the a and b that make the most ATP within its limits (glucose, oxygen, mitochondria). That is flux balance analysis, a standard tool in computational biology. Click a cell to see its point.

The budget

In the budget scenarios, a tumor cell has a fixed protein budget to split between glucose transporters (cost 1 each) and mitochondria (cost 10 each). Fermenting is cheap per ATP made; respiring is efficient per glucose. When sugar is plentiful, fermenting can win; when it is scarce, respiring wins.

What the experiments found

In this model the tumor can only invade if its acid kills healthy cells, and the healthy cells' acid tolerance decides whether it does. Evolution never favored more fermenting: tumors ferment to invade, then their descendants drift back toward respiring. Real tumors keep fermenting, so the model is missing something. Likely candidates: evolving acid tolerance, needing glucose carbon to build new cells, or physically pushing into tissue.
