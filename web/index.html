Why do tumors ferment sugar?

Many cancer cells make energy by fermenting sugar, even with plenty of oxygen around. That is the Warburg effect. Here, a small tumor grows inside healthy tissue, and every cell decides how to make its energy by solving a small linear algebra problem. Pick a question, press Play, and watch.

1. Can a tumor's acid clear out healthy tissue?

These tumor cells ferment almost all their sugar and give off lactate, an acid. Healthy cells die in strong acid; tumor cells can take three times more.

2. Does evolution favor fermenting?

Each tumor cell splits a fixed budget between sugar pumps and mitochondria (the cell's respiration machinery). When a tumor cell divides, its daughter's split changes a little at random. Cells whose split works better leave more descendants.

Watch for: tumor color shifting from orange (fermenting) to purple (respiring), and the bottom chart.

3. What if tumor cells always grab the fastest energy, no matter the cost?

Here every tumor cell re-decides each step how to split its budget, always picking whatever makes the most energy right now. Fermenting is the fastest, so they all switch to it, without "knowing" that their acid builds up around them.

How it works

1. Two ways to burn sugar

A cell can respire one glucose using oxygen in its mitochondria: slow to set up, but it makes about 32 ATP (energy units). Or it can ferment it: fast and cheap, but only 2 ATP, plus 2 lactate (acid) as waste.

2. The cell as a matrix

Write the cell's chemistry as a matrix S: one row per molecule, one column per reaction. A list of reaction speeds v keeps every molecule in balance when Sv = 0. All the balanced choices form the null space of S, and it turns out to be built from exactly two pathways:

ferment = (1, 1, 0, 2, 0, 2)
respire = (1, 1, 2, 0, 2, 0)

So every cell's metabolism is a × ferment + b × respire: a glucose fermented, b respired.

3. Each cell picks the best mix

A cell chooses a and b to make the most ATP within its limits: the glucose it can take in, the oxygen around it, and how much its mitochondria can handle. Biologists call this flux balance analysis. A cell with little oxygen, or small mitochondria, has to ferment.

4. Life on the grid

Capillaries (dark dots) supply oxygen and glucose and wash acid away; everything spreads between squares. Each step, every cell eats and makes ATP. Then it dies if it has too little ATP or too much acid, divides into an empty neighboring square if it has plenty, or otherwise stays.

5. Who wins a free square?

Healthy cells can only grow into empty space. If a tumor cell and a healthy cell both want the same empty square, the tumor wins 3 times out of 4: cancer cells ignore the signals that tell normal cells to stop dividing. Healthy cells can win: they take back space when the tumor dies, and with stronger acid tolerance (Advanced settings) they hold the tumor off.

6. What it found, and its limits

Tumors here invade only when their acid kills healthy cells. Over generations they evolve away from fermenting. Real tumors keep fermenting, so the model is missing something: maybe evolving acid tolerance, or needing sugar's carbon to build new cells. It is a teaching model with simple, made-up numbers in places.
